Every thin arc is a single citation, leaving the paper on the day it was published and landing in the year it was made. Columns are years; their height is how many citations that year brought.

The stamp falls on the year the journal declared the result invalid. Everything pale happened while the paper was still standing. Everything red happened after.

What it shows — this paper was cited 950 times after it was retracted, against 936 before. Half its citation life happened after it was withdrawn.

Where in science this happens

3.4 million biomedical papers, placed by what they are about. Neighbours are papers on the same subject. Pale points are the literature; red points are the retracted ones.

What it shows — the red is not spread evenly. Against a baseline rate of 0.057%, the worst neighbourhood runs at 10.7%, or 188× the average, and half of all retractions sit in 4.3% of the map. Two thirds of neighbourhoods have none at all.

This map is built from text similarity, so it shows that some subjects are far riskier than others — not yet that some research groups are. Those are different graphs.

The ring nobody counts

Every count so far has been of papers citing the retracted paper directly — authors who at least had it in their hands. The tall columns here are the ring beyond that: papers citing one of those. Their authors have no reason to know a retraction sits anywhere in their reference chain.

Both series are drawn at the same scale. The direct citations are the dark slivers along the bottom.

What it shows — direct citation falls after retraction, from 307 a year to 12. Second-hand citation keeps climbing, from 2,926 to 9,318. The year before retraction there were 10 second-hand citations for every direct one. By 2026 there are 396.

The correction reaches the first ring. It never reaches the second.

The number we defend

63% of citations arriving after retraction is the headline, and it is partly an artefact. The median paper here spent two years valid and four years retracted. It simply had longer.

So divide by the time available. Every dot is one paper: citations per year before retraction across, citations per year after it up. The diagonal is the line where nothing changed.

What it shows — most papers fall below the line, so retraction does slow them. The median lands at 0.62×: about a third off the rate. But 63 of 205 sit above it, cited faster dead than alive.

Retraction slows the spread by about a third. It does not stop it.

205 papers, not 300. A rate before retraction needs at least one year of being valid, and 87 of these were retracted in the same year they were published; 8 more were never cited before. Both axes are logarithmic, so each step is ten times the one before.

How it was measured

OpenAlex says which papers are retracted and how many times each was cited in each year. It does not record when a paper was retracted, so the dates come from Retraction Watch via Crossref. Joining those two is what makes a before and an after exist at all.

Only papers published from 2012 on. OpenAlex reports citations per year for roughly the last fifteen years, so an older paper is missing its earliest citations — which are all pre-retraction. Including them would have inflated the posthumous share in our own favour, so they are dropped, and every surviving paper's yearly counts must reconcile with its headline total.

What this does not claim. Retracted is not the same as fraudulent. A second-hand citation is reachability, not influence — the citing paper may be citing its source for something unrelated. The second ring is also structurally later in time, so some of its posthumous share is lag rather than contagion. And area on screen is not count: the number is the number.
